The recruitment landscape has undergone a significant transformation in recent years, driven by the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) technologies. Among these advancements, automated resume screening has emerged as a powerful tool for organizations seeking to streamline their hiring processes. By leveraging AI-driven models, companies can rapidly evaluate large volumes of job applications, identify top candidates, and reduce the time and costs associated with manual screening. This shift toward automation has been particularly beneficial in industries with high applicant turnover, such as technology, retail, and healthcare, where recruiters often face the daunting task of sifting through thousands of resumes for a single job posting. Despite its efficiency and scalability, the use of AI in resume screening has raised significant ethical and practical concerns. One of the most pressing issues is the potential for these systems to perpetuate or even exacerbate historical biases present in the training data. AI models are typically trained on historical hiring data, which may reflect past discriminatory practices or systemic inequalities. For example, if a company has historically favored candidates from certain countries, genders, or age groups, an AI model trained on this data may learn to replicate these biases, leading to unfair and discriminatory outcomes. This is particularly problematic in the context of hiring, where decisions have profound implications for individuals’ careers, livelihoods, and societal mobility. The manifestation of bias in AI-driven hiring systems can take many forms. For instance, a model might disproportionately favor candidates from specific countries while undervaluing equally qualified applicants from underrepresented regions. Similarly, biases related to gender or age may result in the exclusion of certain demographic groups, even when their qualifications align with the job requirements. These biases not only undermine the principles of fairness and equity but also expose organizations to legal, reputational, and financial risks. In recent years, several high-profile cases have highlighted the dangers of biased AI systems, prompting calls for greater transparency, accountability, and fairness in automated decisionmaking. Against this backdrop, this study seeks to address the following research questions:
      a) To what extent do AI-driven resume screening models exhibit bias based on demographic attributes? We aim to quantify the level of bias in a baseline employability prediction model, with a particular focus on country-based bias, which emerged as a significant factor in our preliminary analyses.
      b) How effective are different bias mitigation techniques (pre-, in-, and post-processing) in reducing this bias? We evaluate multiple debiasing strategies, including Disparate Impact Remover, Reweighing, and Adversarial Debiasing, to determine their efficacy in promoting  fairness.
      c) What is the impact of debiasing on model performance and interpretability? We examine the trade-offs between fairness and accuracy, as well as the implications of debiasing for model interpretability, using tools such as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations).

This study focuses primarily on country-based bias, as it was found to have a pronounced effect on the model’s predictions. By concentrating on this attribute, we aim to provide a detailed and nuanced analysis of how bias manifests in AI-driven hiring systems and how it can be effectively mitigated. Additionally, we explore the broader implications of our findings for the development and deployment of fairness-aware AI systems in recruitment. The significance of this research lies in its potential to inform best practices for the ethical use of AI in hiring. As organizations increasingly rely on automated systems to make critical decisions, it is essential to ensure that these systems are not only accurate but also fair and unbiased. By quantifying bias, evaluating mitigation techniques, and analyzing their impact on model performance, this study contributes to the growing body of literature on AI fairness and provides actionable insights for practitioners. Furthermore, this research highlights the importance of interpretability in AI systems. While debiasing techniques can reduce bias, they may also alter the way models make decisions. 
Understanding these changes is crucial for ensuring transparency and accountability. To this end, we employ SHAP analysis to examine shifts in feature importance before and after debiasing, providing valuable insights into how sensitive attributes influence model predictions. In summary, this study addresses a critical gap in the literature by combining bias quantification, debiasing techniques, and interpretability analysis in the context of AI-driven resume screening. Our findings have the potential to shape the future of ethical AI in recruitment, ensuring that automated systems promote fairness, equity, and inclusivity in hiring practices.
